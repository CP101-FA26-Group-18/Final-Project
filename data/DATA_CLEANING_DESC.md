Our primary dataset is the US Census Bureau’s Longitudinal Employer-Household Dynamics (LEHD) Origin-Destination Employment Statistics (LODES8), which provides annual block-level origin-destination (OD) commuter counts from 2017 through 2023. Because data is organized by state, we used the California-specific datasets for all job types (JT00) that explicitly exclude commuters that cross state lines. 

We filtered the yearly datasets to keep only the OD pairs where both the workplace and residence (or home) census blocks are within the nine Bay Area counties,which dropped roughly 80% of statewide records overall. For example, the number of OD pair entries in the 2023 dataset dropped by roughly 13.27 million rows (81.70%) from 16.24 million to 2.97 million. Removing these entries was necessary as we will focus our analysis of daytime population trends on just commuters within the nine county Bay Area. Then, the remaining block pairs were grouped into census tract OD pairs using 11-digit census FIPS codes. This compressed each annual dataset by approximately 74% (e.g., 767,054 pairs in 2023) while preserving all of the underlying commuter volume (e.g., sum of 3,288,681 jobs in 2023). Preserving all commuter volume was done by summing the commuter counts (S000 column) where grouping operations took place. Aggregating to the county level was also done in a similar manner.

Grouping census blocks to the higher granularity of tracts was deemed necessary for two main reasons: First, block-level data is known to be noisy, thus risking overplotting in maps [insert source here]. Second, it allows us to overcome RAM constraints we ran into trying to load block-level files in Python. A single year tract-level dataset takes around 1 GB of memory in our environment yet is about 25% of its corresponding block-level dataset size. We also have to consider that our complete analysis consists of 7 yearly datasets. Thus, having our finest granularity at the tract level is simply much more practical.

For our city-level groupings, we used the 2023 US Census TIGER/Line shapefiles for California Census Tracts and Places. Out of the 9,128 statewide census tracts, we dropped 7,356 non-Bay Area tracts (80.6%) and only kept the 1,772 tracts within the nine Bay Area counties. Because census tracts and municipal boundaries do not naturally match each other, we reprojected both geodataframes to EPSG:2227 (NAD83 / California State Plane Zone 3, in US Survey Feet) to perform accurate tract-to-city classifications.

To group tracts into cities without double-counting any tracts, we applied the largest-share rule from Lab 9 using gpd.overlay(how=“intersection”), assigning each tract to the single city or Census Designated Place (CDP) that overlaps the most with the tract’s land area. This produced a clean one-to-one mapping of 1,767 Bay Area tracts to 179 unique municipalities. The remaining 5 tracts were outside any official place boundary, so we categorized them as “Unincorporated” rather than forcing them into an adjacent city. This is intended to avoid artificially inflating neighboring municipalities’ commuter counts.

Using this, we grouped our tract OD pairs into city-level OD pairs, reducing the data to between 21,795 and 22,336 city pairs annually. Lastly, we ran a verification check to make sure no commuters were accidentally dropped during these spatial groupings, confirming that total commuter sums matched perfectly across the tract, city, and county levels (e.g., exactly 3,288,681 commuters in 2023).

To supplement our commuter flows with remote-work trends, we used the US Census Bureau’s American Community Survey (ACS) 1-year estimates from Table B08301 (Means of Transportation to Work). We gathered data for the nine Bay Area counties across 2017 through 2019 and 2021 through 2023. Data for 2020 is excluded because of the Census Bureau pausing its standard 1-year releases that year due to pandemic-related disruptions. We cleaned each yearly file by removing the descriptive header row, dropping all 21 margin-of-error columns (as we are only interested in the actual estimates), and slicing the 14-digit geographic IDs down to the 5-digit FIPS codes for the 9 Bay Area counties. We then mapped these FIPS codes to their county names and calculated the work-from-home (WFH) rate by dividing the number of remote workers (B08301_021E) by total workers (B08301_001E). 

Because Napa County (FIPS=06055) had a sample size too small to meet the Census publication threshold in 2021 and 2022, its raw entries for those years are missing. We fixed this issue by placing NaN entries for those two data points rather than interpolating values to avoid skewing the data. Using ACS WFH rates strictly at the county level allows us to avoid the severe margins of error and 5-year smoothing issues found in tract-level ACS samples. This gives us a set of regional WFH benchmarks that we can incorporate into our analysis of daytime population trends before and after the Covid-19 pandemic.
